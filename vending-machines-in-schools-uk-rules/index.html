Vending Machines in Schools: UK Rules and Healthy Options

Vending machines in schools sit inside the school food rules of England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland. This guide covers snacks, drinks, staff rooms and pupil areas. The managed service is school vending machines. The range is healthy vending machines.

England: School Food Standards

Compliance is mandatory for maintained schools, academies and free schools. The standards in force came in on 1 January 2015 under the Requirements for School Food Regulations 2014. School food in England is the Department for Education’s guide.

Lunch standards apply to registered pupils and to any other person on the premises. Food and drink other than lunch, provided to pupils up to 6pm, is also covered, including vending. Parties, fundraising, rewards, cookery lessons and food brought in occasionally are outside the standards.

The checklist for school food other than lunch has a vending column. Fruit and vegetables must be available in every outlet. Confectionery, chocolate and chocolate-coated products are out, as are savoury crackers and breadsticks. Snacks are limited to nuts, seeds, vegetables and fruit with no added salt, sugar or fat. Cakes, biscuits, pastries and desserts are out, except yoghurt or fruit-based desserts with at least 50% fruit. The school food standards limit drinks to plain water, lower-fat or lactose-reduced milk, fruit or vegetable juice up to 150ml, and listed milk drinks and combination drinks up to 330ml. Free, fresh drinking water must be available at all times. Proposed updates from September 2027 are not in force.

Scotland: food and drink standards, including vending

The Nutritional Requirements for Food and Drink in Schools (Scotland) Regulations 2020 came into force on 8 April 2021. Scottish Government guidance says the food and drink standards apply to food and drink provided to children and young people at any time of day, including tuck shops and vending machines. Nutrient standards apply to specified meals, not to every vending line.

No confectionery at any time, including a chocolate bar from a machine. Fruit or vegetables must be available wherever food is provided. Outside the primary lunch, plain crackers, oatcakes and breadsticks are allowed, and other pre-packed savoury snacks only when they meet the primary criteria. Primary drinks are water, lower-fat milk, lower-fat calcium-enriched milk alternatives, and no-added-sugar lower-fat milk drinks and drinking yoghurts. Secondary schools may also serve tea, coffee and sugar-free drinks that are not high-caffeine (150mg per litre). Check the allergy policy before nuts.

Wales: maintained schools, with a change for primary on 31 October 2026

The Healthy Eating in Schools (Nutritional Standards and Requirements) (Wales) Regulations 2013 apply where a local authority or governing body provides food or drink to pupils on a school day before 6pm, and to any other person on the premises before 6pm. Statutory guidance gives staff as an example of that wider group, and it names vending machines under the rules for food outside breakfast and lunch.

Confectionery is not permitted, nor are savoury snacks as the regulations define them, including crisps and similar potato, cereal and rice-cake products. Fruit and vegetables must be available wherever food is provided. Primary break-time snacks should be fruit and vegetables only. Secondary cakes and biscuits are allowed only with no confectionery, and only as part of the nutritional analysis of lunch. Food in a vending machine follows those other-food standards. If the machine provides lunch, that food is analysed with the rest of the lunch.

Welsh Government guidance keeps the 2013 regulations for maintained secondary schools and for learners in year 7 and above. They cease to apply to maintained nursery and primary schools, and to learners in year 6 and below, on 31 October 2026. From that date the 2025 primary regulations apply to food and drink provided to learners, including from a vending machine. A primary school changing a machine around that date should read the 2025 guidance with its caterer.

Northern Ireland: nutritional standards for other food and drinks

In grant-aided schools, the standards for other food and drinks apply to vending machines, tuck shops, breakfast clubs, after-school clubs and break times. The Department of Education’s nutritional standards page says the current standards stay in place, and links the implementation guides. Governors are expected to keep vending and tuck shops to the standards:

  • No confectionery should be sold in schools.
  • No savoury snacks should be sold, other than nuts and seeds without added salt or sugar. Nuts are a decision for the school because of allergies.
  • A variety of fruit and vegetables should be available in all school food outlets. Dried fruit is better as part of a meal than as a snack between meals, because the sugars are more concentrated.
  • Children and young people must have easy access at all times to free, fresh, preferably chilled water.
  • The only other drinks should be bottled water (still or sparkling), milk, unsweetened fruit or vegetable juices, yoghurt and milk drinks with less than 5% added sugar, or drinks made from combinations of those (such as smoothies), plus low-calorie hot chocolate, tea and coffee. Combination drinks must be at least 50% milk, yoghurt or fruit juice, as appropriate. Artificial sweeteners are only permitted in combination drinks. Tea, coffee and low-calorie hot chocolate are not suitable for the nursery sector.

That wording is the Department’s standards as restated in the arrangements in force from 7 June 2024.

What that means for snacks and drinks in a school machine

A workplace mix of chocolate and crisps is the wrong start for pupils. Drinks come from that nation’s permitted list, and free drinking water is a separate duty. The menu is agreed for the machine. VendingNI’s healthy vending range, and the way people pay, follow the school’s own rules.

Staff rooms and pupil areas

A staff-only machine is not the same question in each nation.

  • England. Vending is covered when food other than lunch is provided to pupils up to 6pm. A room pupils cannot use sits outside that wording. Lunch standards still apply to any other person on the premises.
  • Scotland. The standards apply to food provided to children and young people. A machine they cannot reach is outside that wording, including where a staff room is not fully closed off.
  • Wales. The 2013 regulations cover any other person on the premises before 6pm, and the guidance names staff. From 31 October 2026 a primary school should read the 2025 guidance, which is written around food provided to learners.
  • Northern Ireland. The standards are written for vending machines in schools and do not separate a staff machine.

Healthy options

Healthy options are the lines the standards allow for that age group: water, milk, fruit, vegetables and permitted snacks. A workplace mix is a poor template for a pupil area. Where a site qualifies, school vending machines are fully managed, the range is agreed with the school, and the school remains responsible for the rules.

Questions a school should ask

Who may use the machine?

Say whether pupils, staff or both can reach it. The staff-room section above is the difference between the four nations. The provider needs that fact before the range is agreed.

Which nation’s rules apply?

England uses the 2014 school food regulations. Scotland uses the 2020 nutritional requirements regulations. Wales uses the 2013 regulations, and the 2025 primary regulations from 31 October 2026. Northern Ireland uses the Department of Education nutritional standards for other food and drinks. A college should check whether those school rules apply to it.

What can the machine sell?

The school sets the range. Confectionery is restricted in every nation’s vending rules. Drinks come from that nation’s permitted list. Fruit and vegetables have to be available where the standards require them. Nuts follow the allergy policy.

Who checks the range still matches the rules?

The governing body, academy trust, local authority or education authority remains responsible, including where a caterer fills the machine. On a managed VendingNI machine, replenishment and product changes follow the rules the school has set.
